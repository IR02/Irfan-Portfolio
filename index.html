Low-Cost Acoustic Sensing & Data Acquisition for Cryogenic Carbon Fibre Reinforced Polymer

Compared a £26 piezoelectric disc and Arduino Uno R3 with a laboratory IEPE accelerometer, PCB 482A22 conditioner and PicoScope 4824A on woven carbon fibre reinforced polymer coupons. Twenty-two specimens were made and a motorised liquid-nitrogen dipping rig was built. Both channels detected five out of five controlled mechanical taps. The low-cost channel is a proof of concept for envelope-level event detection, not a substitute for commercial acoustic-emission equipment.

Hardware. The reference channel was a PCB Piezotronics ICP accelerometer, wax-mounted, powered by a PCB 482A22 conditioner at 4 mA and digitised on a PicoScope 4824A (12-bit, ±5 V, 25 kHz on the ambient test). The prototype channel was a 27 mm piezoelectric disc, Araldite-bonded at the opposite end and read on Arduino Uno R3 pin A3 (10-bit, 0–5 V) at about 500 Hz. A TL072 chain, with AC coupling, a 2.5 V bias, a gain of 101 and a diode clamp, was built on breadboard and then bypassed: the bias rail drifted and the high-impedance input picked up mains. The disc was connected straight to the ADC, so negative half-cycles clip to zero.

Carbon fibre reinforced polymer manufacture. Two 380 × 380 mm panels were laid up by hand from eight plies of woven 0/90 carbon/epoxy prepreg and cured to 1.75 mm. They were machined into 22 coupons, 350 × 25 × 1.75 mm, sized for later flexural and tensile geometries. This was an 8-ply woven layup, not a quasi-isotropic laminate.

Automated LN₂ rig. An MDF frame carried a brushed DC motor, a 40 mm spool and a nylon cord. An L298N H-bridge drove the motor. An HC-SR04 measured hook position to about ±3 mm, and a DHT11 logged ambient temperature and humidity. Arduino firmware lowered the coupon, held the dwell, then raised it. One pilot dip was completed: about 100 mm submerged for five minutes in a 5 L Dewar. That was not a thermal-fatigue campaign.

Data processing. Both records were processed in Python with NumPy, pandas and Matplotlib: mean removal, a Hann window, a one-sided FFT, magnitude scaling by 2/Σw, then magnitude averaging across the five PicoScope captures. Frequency resolution on the reference captures was about 19.9 Hz per bin.

Quantitative results. All five screwdriver taps were detected on both channels. Reference signal-to-noise ratio at the peak was above 30 dB; the Arduino was above 20 dB. These were controlled mechanical taps, not five confirmed damage events. Peak-amplitude coefficient of variation was 29% on the reference and 43% on the low-cost channel. The averaged spectrum had a dominant peak near 3.3 kHz (about 3349 Hz), with smaller peaks near 1.7 kHz and 2.2 kHz. That peak is the specimen–sensor mechanical response, not a confirmed damage frequency. Acoustic-emission damage bands for carbon fibre reinforced polymer sit far higher. At about 500 Hz the Arduino Nyquist limit is 250 Hz, so it records the envelope rather than the 3.3 kHz oscillation.

Pilot cryogenic dip. The PicoScope, triggered at 0.4 V, recorded no window during the five-minute dwell. The Arduino trace contained two bursts, with peaks of 801 and 576 counts against a quiet baseline scatter of about 0.75 counts. Those two events were not identified as carbon fibre reinforced polymer damage: boil-off, rig motion and a partial wire detachment are also plausible, and no cracking was visible afterwards. During this test the Arduino rate fell to about 48 Hz because extra sensor columns were logged on the serial line.

Quantum Heist

A creative and engaging quantum hackathon project that transforms the weighted Max-Cut problem into a realistic bank robbery scenario. The bank's vault network is modeled as a 5-node graph where nodes represent vaults and edges represent guarded pathways or critical alarm triggers.

By assigning an extremely high weight of 10,000 to the alarm edge between nodes 3 and 4, we ensure QAOA always includes this edge in the cut, forcing the decoy team to pass through and silently disable the main alarm system. High weights on other edges (such as 1-2) mark the locations of elite security guards that must be neutralized first, allowing the main robbery crew to reach the vaults undetected.

Running QAOA produces a clear sampling histogram with four dominant bitstrings (10010, 01101, 10101, 01010). Although the global maximum cut creates two balanced partitions, we discovered that the near-optimal solutions with exactly two 1s naturally divide the bank into three disconnected regions. This enables three simultaneous strike teams to enter and clear multiple vaults in parallel, significantly reducing total heist time. Our detailed post-processing, strategic insight, and clear visualizations of both the weighted graph and final partition elevate a standard QAOA implementation into a compelling, mission-optimized quantum-powered heist plan.

PLC-Controlled Industrial Sorting, Monitoring and Temperature Regulation

Programmed a Bytronic training system in Siemens TIA Portal, using PLC ladder logic and a WinCC HMI, to sort metal pegs and plastic rings, count components and finished assemblies, stop the conveyor when the line was idle, raise production and rejection alerts, and regulate an oven with a manually tuned PID controller.

Problem and objective. The rig sorts metal pegs and plastic rings, moves them on a belt and feeds an oven. The task was to extend the PLC program so the line could count parts, stop an empty conveyor, tell the operator when production or rejection targets were reached, and hold oven temperature with a commissioned PID loop.

Hardware and software. The program was written in Siemens TIA Portal as ladder logic, with counting logic in the function block Counting [FB4] and operator screens in WinCC. The Bytronic training system provided the inductive and capacitive sensors, sort and reject solenoids, belt conveyor outputs and the industrial training oven.

Counting. Three CTU count-up counters sit inside Counting [FB4]. The plastic-ring counter is triggered by the sort-solenoid output, the metal-peg counter by the inductive sensor, and the assembled-product counter by the capacitive sensor. Presets were 5, 5 and 3. Live values were bound to three fields on the System Control HMI, with one shared reset. Triggering each counter from the signal that actually marks that event avoids counting a part twice, or counting a part that has only passed a different station.

Idle conveyor. A 25-second on-delay timer interlocks the belt. If no new component is detected in that interval, the conveyor stops. Detecting another component resets the timer and lets the belt start again. This is idle-time, energy-conscious automation. The report does not measure an energy saving, so none is claimed.

HMI alerts. The alarm screen reports two conditions: the production target of 3 assembled products, and the rejection-counter threshold of 3. The rejection counter watches reject-solenoid activations, whatever the component type. It is not a validated inventory check. One combined reject count is a weak way to judge remaining usable stock, because a rejected ring and a rejected peg do not change the two part stocks in the same way. Separate rejected-ring and rejected-peg counters would make that logic sound.

PID control. A PID_Compact controller was tuned by hand in TIA Portal: proportional gain Kp = 20, integral time Ti = 50 s, derivative time Td = 10 s, derivative delay coefficient = 0.2, and a sampling time of 1 s. The commissioning trend tracked a 35°C setpoint with residual oscillations of about ±2°C. Raising proportional gain speeds the response and also increases overshoot and oscillation. Integral action removes a steady offset, and too much of it prolongs that oscillation. Derivative action damps how fast the error is changing, but on a temperature measurement it has to be limited, which is the role of the delay coefficient. The loop followed the setpoint. It was not perfect temperature control.

Oven arrival interlock. The capacitive sensor detects an assembled part. A 1.5-second timer represents the travel delay to the oven, and when it expires the PLC inhibits both conveyor outputs so the part is not carried past the oven. This conveyor inhibit was implemented and tested.

Limitations and next steps. Siemens automatic PID pretuning and fine-tuning did not finish, which is why the gains above were entered manually. A three-stage programme of 35°C, 50°C and 40°C, each held for 300 seconds, was proposed and was not built or tested. A 45°C heat-up with a 10-second soak before the conveyor restarted was also not implemented. HMI data logging, simpler TOF/TP timer logic, and extra diagnostic alarms were recorded as improvements only.

CFD Analysis of Turbulent Flow over a Backward-Facing Step

A high-fidelity 2D CFD investigation of incompressible turbulent flow over a classic backward-facing step benchmark (Driver & Seegmiller, 1985) was performed using ANSYS Fluent. The computational domain was carefully designed with an optimized inlet length of 0.76 m to develop a fully turbulent boundary layer matching the experimental thickness of 19 mm at 4 step heights upstream of the expansion.

A structured mesh with 20 inflation layers (first cell height ensuring y⁺ < 1 everywhere), growth rate 1.36, and localized face sizing delivered excellent near-wall resolution and mesh quality (average aspect ratio 1.11).

The k-ω Shear Stress Transport (SST) model with Low-Reynolds correction and production limiter was selected for its proven superiority in predicting separation and reattachment under adverse pressure gradients. A Comparative Vibrational Analysis on Three Different Steam Turbine Blades

Modal analysis was performed on three steam turbine blades using tap tests at ten points arranged in a 5×2 grid. Frequency response functions were computed from five averaged impacts per point.

The first five natural frequencies were extracted as follows: Blade 1 (120.25–644.5 Hz), Blade 2 (116.75–645.75 Hz), and Blade 3 (112.00–650.75 Hz), confirming Blade 1 was stiffest and Blade 3 most flexible. Blade 2 was intermediate, with a first-mode frequency ~7% below Blade 1. Modes were consistent in shape, but Blade 2 and 3 showed slightly lower frequency integrity.


Damping ratios, determined via the half-power method, ranged from 0.025 to 0.0021 for Blade 1 and showed similar trends in Blades 2 and 3. Blade 2 exhibited the highest damping and greater root motion, indicating potential wear, softer boundary conditions, or friction-related energy dissipation. MAC values exceeded 0.9 in modes 1–3, confirming good mode shape agreement. No processing errors were evident; observed variations are attributed to physical differences in structure, support, or operational wear.


Low damping (ζ ~0.002–0.003) implies high Q-factors (~20–40), enabling resonance to amplify vibratory strain by up to 40×. This poses a significant fatigue risk, as a 2× increase in stress amplitude can reduce life by 10×. Detuning from resonance particularly near Blade 3's first mode at 112 Hz is essential. These results inform blade selection, quality control (e.g. MAC < 0.9 or >5% frequency deviation), finite element model validation, and structural monitoring for recommissioning.
